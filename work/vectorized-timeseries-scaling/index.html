Scaling an energy anomaly-detection pipeline

Rewrote the data preparation of a prize-winning time-series pipeline with vectorised group transforms: 72% faster, 1.4 GB less peak memory, and a fitting bug fixed along the way.

Problem

The pipeline is the winning solution to the Large-scale Energy Anomaly Detection (LEAD) competition by Chun Fu, Pandarasamy Arjunan and Clayton Miller (BuildSys '22). It classifies anomalies in hourly building-energy meter readings with a LightGBM, XGBoost, CatBoost and HistGradientBoosting ensemble.

It runs well on the benchmark's few hundred meters, but a practical deployment needed around 2,000 streams. Profiling showed the models were not the bottleneck: the data preparation was. Three steps iterated in Python over every building, sliced a sub-DataFrame, transformed it and stitched the pieces back together.

Approach

  1. 01

    Mean imputation without the loop

    The original filled missing readings building by building, re-filtering the whole frame on every iteration. A single groupby().transform() broadcasts each building's mean back to its rows in one pass.

  2. 02

    Savitzky-Golay smoothing per group

    The smoothing residual is a key anomaly feature. Instead of appending one DataFrame per building and concatenating thousands of them, the filter runs inside a group transform, so no temporary frames are created and destroyed.

  3. 03

    One merge per lag instead of two

    Lag features are built for 60 offsets, from one hour up to a week either side. The original made two full passes, one for difference features and one for ratio features, each re-reading the CSV and converting timestamps to strings on every merge. The rewrite parses timestamps once and computes both features from a single merge per offset.

Results

Benchmarked end to end on a standard Kaggle Linux container with an automated resource tracker. The vectorised version finished in 7.3 minutes instead of 26.6 and peaked 1.4 GB lower, because it stops allocating temporary DataFrames inside Python loops; the arithmetic runs on pandas' compiled backend over contiguous arrays, the design argument in Wes McKinney's original pandas paper.

The goal was strict parity in accuracy. While checking parity I found the iterative version was fitting on subsets of the data; with that fixed, the private leaderboard score rose from 0.933 to 0.952.

My role

Solo project. I reproduced the published solution on Kaggle, profiled it, rewrote the preprocessing, built the benchmark harness and verified accuracy parity.
